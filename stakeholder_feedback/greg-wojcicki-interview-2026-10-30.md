## Summary

1. **What makes an RFI "hot":** it depends on whether the sub who raised it is blocked. If they have 10 other things to work on, it can wait. If it's the only thing they have going, it's urgent. Renovation work inside existing campus buildings adds a lot of surprises found only after opening a wall.
2. **Where time is lost:** the architect is a funnel. Engineering questions get forwarded to the mechanical, electrical or structural engineer, adding a step, and pickup depends on people's workload (an email may be seen the same day or a day later). The working norm is "assume about a week for an answer"; hot ones get escalated by phone.
3. **Early routing to the right responder:** helpful, since "the sooner it gets into their hands, the sooner they can respond." There's no draft RFI stage, so an "early copy" doesn't really exist. His idea: a checkbox in the contractor's system (Procore, Prolog, etc.) that sends the RFI to the engineer at the same time as the architect.
4. **Cost vs. time:** every RFI has two parts: getting an answer, and getting it on time so work isn't delayed. Cost pulls in the owner, but on design-build (like Haggett Hall) the design-builder holds the budget and contingency, so the owner often approves the work first ("go add the wall") and settles the dollar amount later.
5. **Referrals:** Andrew, the Haggett Hall architect who handled many of the RFIs (back in office next week); Greg will email an intro. He may also connect us with the team on the Suzzallo Library seismic project (GLY Construction, the general contractor), who he says should already be getting information back to us.
