01 · TEAM THESIS

Team thesis

When AI is used to rewrite or summarize words in a Request for Information (RFI), it will alter the original meaning of the request, these alterations being numbers rounded, words being construed, decision branches being dropped, and jargon and human specificity being lost. We’ll test this by comparing each original RFI with its AI-written version. This can create an RFI that communicates something different from the problem identified on the project. Construction information flow breaks down at certain points of translation. For example, an issue can be noticed by a subcontractor, then a general contractor can formalize the issue as an RFI, which then gets interpreted by the respective entities on the project. Keeping the correct parties in the loop in an RFI minimizes the chance of information getting lost in translation or not correctly passed along later (The RFI In Construction: What It Is, What It's For & 7 Key Best Practices, 2020). Each iteration of this process and RFI creation is a chance for the issue's meaning to change if AI is used. The purpose of an RFI is to fill in information gaps at request by capturing and sharing specific decisions made during the project (Medina and Tell, 2025), so an RFI that does the opposite by miswording the original issue defeats its own purpose. This can lead to a response to the wrong questions, trigger an unnecessary clarifying follow-up RFI, or lead to a change that wasn't intended. This matters because RFIs are already costly and slow. According to a 2013 Navigant study, managing/responding to one RFI costs an average of $1,080, with response times averaging 6.4 to 10 days. This shows that nearly 25% of all RFIs go completely unanswered (as cited in Medina and Tell, 2025).

We are specifically focusing on the creation stage of the RFI lifecycle because those early-stage errors can be carried into the review, response, approval, and implementation stages. Upon our analysis of the RFI workflow, we identified five likely key failures with AI usage. The first is that numbers could be rounded or overclaimed, which changes quantities and dimensions of the project, and dimension and quantity discrepancies are already a common reason for an RFI (The RFI In Construction: What It Is, What It's For & 7 Key Best Practices, June 18, 2020). Second, words are misconstrued, meaning the output has a different meaning than the input. Third, decision branches are dropped, so there's a risk of loss of detail where, if you drop too many branches, the LLM becomes too simple and open-ended questions can be scoped down to a singular answer. Fourth, jargon and human specificity are lost. RFIs should reference specific drawings and specifications and describe exactly where the information is lacking. Finally, prior RFI creation with the same LLM can have effects on new ones by pulling context from earlier requests that can be incorrect or non-relevant information into the new and current RFI. These failures are extremely important to address because an RFI is used as technical information for construction implementation and part of the official project record (Medina and Tell, 2025). An inaccurate RFI can cause schedule delays, unapproved changes, and cost disputes in materials and billing/labor. Apart from technical project issues, it also creates an accountability problem because if the inaccuracy starts at the drafting stage, then it could be very hard to trace where the issue actually began. AI cannot be held responsible for it, so automatically the general contractor would be held accountable for being the RFI author.

02 · PROJECT LENS

Project lens

Our team will use the public RFI log from the Brush Creek Work Center Construction solicitation (127EAW26R0001), issued by the U.S. Forest Service. The log is dated March 27th, 2026 and it contains 271 questions from bidders and their respective government responses. These are pre-award questions from bidders to the government, not construction-phase RFIs that have been passed from the subcontractors then through to a GC then to a design team. However, this log still fits our study. Each of the questions is a real contractor's own wording regarding drawings, specifications and scope so it works as a representation of the original issue that a GC would formalize. The government's response will alos give us a reference answer to check whether a question that is rewritten would get a similar response. Many entries have to do with proposal logic, like page limits and past performance. We can easily filter this to just technical questions and select the 10 that best cover our demos (listed below). We do not plan to use synthetic data. If a test will require it, such as ambiguity, we will instead just modify a real RFI and keep its construction context.

03 · COORDINATION POSTURE

Coordination posture

Parties involved:

Subcontractor / Contractor — identifies the original construction issue and communicates the request for clarification.

General Contractor (GC) — reviews the issue, uses AI assistance to rewrite or structure the request, verifies that the original meaning is preserved, and submits the formal RFI.

Design Team / Owner Representative — receives the RFI, interprets the request, and provides clarification or a response.

Information artifacts in scope:

The main information artifact is the Request for Information (RFI). Additional artifacts might be the original contractor question, project drawings, specifications, and additional artifacts may include the original contractor question, relevant drawings, specifications, and supporting project documents needed to interpret the RFI.

Coordination posture:

AI will be used only as a drafting and organization assistant for RFIs. It may improve clarity, grammar, structure, and conciseness, but the human user remains responsible for verifying the rewritten RFI before submission. AI should not change the original intent, introduce unsupported assumptions, resolve ambiguity on its own, or make design or scope decisions.

04 · DEMO CONCEPTS

Demo Concepts

01

Demo 1 — Basic RFI Rewrite

Test whether AI can rewrite a straightforward RFI for clarity without changing its original meaning.

02

Demo 2 — Context Preservation

Test whether AI preserves important contextual details when rewriting a longer and more detailed RFI.

03

Demo 3 — Ambiguity Preservation

Test whether AI preserves uncertainty in an ambiguous RFI instead of introducing its own interpretation.

04

Demo 4 — Meaning Verification

Test whether a human verification step comparing the original and AI-rewritten RFI reduces meaning distortion.
